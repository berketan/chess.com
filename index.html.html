Scouting Report

Product taste, in the chess context. Not just claimed.

Online and over-the-board aren't the same product wearing different skins. A misclick online costs you a piece; a shaking hand across a real board in a real tournament costs you something else entirely. That difference is a big part of why Chess.com's growth model works: you can play a thousand games and enter a hundred tournaments without one of them touching your official FIDE rating, and for a huge number of players, especially anyone who plays better unwatched, that's not a compromise. It's the whole appeal. The product isn't just chess. It's chess with the pressure engineered out until you ask for it back.

A beginner and an upper-intermediate player aren't using the same Chess.com, even on the same page. Beginners have more to engage with (more lessons, more guided content, more reasons to open the app), and that's a real driver of overall engagement, not a side effect. It's also why Chess.com ratings and FIDE ratings for the same player rarely match: more games played, nobody watching, being at home instead of across from a stranger. All of it changes how a player performs, not just how they feel about it.

One more pattern worth having an opinion on: strong players online mostly avoid classical time controls: too few opponents at that level, and nobody wants to stare at a screen for three hours for one game. They live in blitz and bullet instead. It's the same reason streamers play bullet almost exclusively: a thirty-minute think is a content killer, and viewers want the next move now. Not a criticism. A real constraint on how any feature aimed at "serious" players actually gets used, worth designing around rather than assuming away.
